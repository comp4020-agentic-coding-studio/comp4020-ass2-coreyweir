# Graphics

Week 11

---

{/* _id: constraint */}

## Where an approach stops being a choice

By the time a program needs to draw, the route you took to get it running has
already decided what it is allowed to draw with.

Every other week in this course has been about options. This one is about
consequences, and the four routes lead to four quite different answers — none
of which is a preference you get to express.

---

{/* _id: compiled */}

## You compiled the application

You get the browser's own graphics APIs, and you rewrite whatever your program
used to call.

The constraints arrive together: **one drawing context per surface**, no
sharing between them, and a subset of the API you knew. That last one is why
the widget every desktop toolkit provides for "put a drawing context in a
window" is unavailable in the web build.

The rewrite is the port. Not a step in it.

---

## But the scope is wider than it first looks

You are not limited to compiling the *application*.

Somebody has compiled an entire **X server** to WebAssembly.

So "port the app" and "port the thing the app talks to" are both on the table,
and the second one ports every client at once — which is a completely
different cost curve, and the kind of move that only becomes visible when you
stop treating the application as the unit.

---

{/* _id: posix */}

## You compiled against a POSIX surface

Your program still believes it is opening a socket and speaking a display
protocol. So give it something to speak to.

A **Wayland compositor written in TypeScript** will do exactly that: each
client surface becomes a WebGL texture, one canvas is one Wayland output, and
clients reach it over a message channel rather than a Unix socket.

Which is elegant, and has one condition attached.

---

## The condition

The client has to be **rebuilt** to reach it. There is no Unix socket, so
there is no unmodified client.

Which makes this cheaper than it sounds only if you were rebuilding anyway —
and if you were rebuilding anyway, compare it honestly against just targeting
the browser's graphics APIs directly.

The interesting case is when you have many clients and one protocol. Then the
compositor is written once.

---

{/* _id: emulated */}

## You emulated a machine

You get a **framebuffer**, blitted to a canvas.

Unaccelerated. Completely general. Indifferent to what the guest thinks it is
doing.

It is why a twenty-five-year-old desktop works perfectly in a tab, and why a
modern one does not: the guest is doing all its compositing in software on an
emulated CPU, and then you copy the result.

The oldest answer, and still the only one that needs no cooperation from the
guest at all.

---

{/* _id: kernel */}

## You ran a kernel

Implement the graphics device and the kernel's mode-setting interface, and an
**unmodified X server** will drive it. No patches, no rebuild, no shim — the
driver interface is the contract, and you implemented the contract.

In two dimensions.

Wayland stays out of reach while the EGL layer is unimplemented, and that is
not a small remaining task. It is the accelerated path.

---

## Four routes, four answers

| You did this        | You draw with                    | Unmodified client? |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------ |
| compiled the app    | the browser's graphics APIs      | no — you rewrote it |
| compiled the server | the same, once, for every client | yes                |
| a POSIX surface     | a compositor in the page         | no — rebuild required |
| emulated a machine  | a framebuffer                    | yes                |
| ran a kernel        | mode-setting, 2D                 | yes                |

The column that decides your semester is the third one.

{/* _id: remote */}

## One detail from outside the premise

If you are permitted *not* to run the program in the browser, the whole
problem changes: keep it on a real machine, and ship pixels.

That same Wayland compositor has a remote path. A real compositor on a Linux
host, surfaces captured zero-copy from the graphics stack, encoded as
**H.264**, sent over a WebSocket, decoded by the browser's own video decoder.

Which is mature, well-understood technology, and then this happens.

---

{/* _class: impact */}

# H.264 has no alpha channel.

---

## So every frame is sent twice

Once for colour, once for opacity, as **two separate video streams**,
recomposited in the page.

A window with a rounded corner or a drop shadow is not decoration here — it is
a second encoder, a second decoder, and twice the bandwidth.

A constraint three layers down, in a codec chosen in 2003 for broadcast
television, reached up and changed the architecture of a compositor written
for the web.

---

## Which is the week's actual lesson

Nobody chose that. It was not a trade-off anyone deliberated over; it fell out
of a format decision made by people who were not thinking about window
managers.

The rendering path is full of these, and they are all discovered late, because
drawing is the last thing a port gets to.

That is why the honest time to cost graphics is **before you can draw
anything** — when it is still a paragraph in a characterisation rather than a
rewrite.

---

## Before the session

Establish the rendering path your approach has already committed you to, and
then find its first hard edge.

Not "is it slow". Find something your target does — a second window, a shared
context, a resize, transparency, a cursor — that the path you are on **cannot
express**, and work out what it would take.

Bring the thing that cannot be expressed. Those are more useful than
benchmarks this week.
